1. Introduction & Research Objectives

Life expectancy at birth is one of the most fundamental indicators of national well-being, reflecting population health status, sanitary infrastructure, nutritional availability, and general social progress. While life expectancy has improved substantially over the past half-century, profound disparities remain between different geographic regions and economic classes.

The primary objective of this project is to conduct an empirical, descriptive analysis examining cross-country relationships between life expectancy and key socioeconomic drivers: national economic output (GDP per capita), public healthcare financing (% of GDP allocated to health), and human capital development (adult literacy rate). This study analyzes a standardized dataset of 50 nations to identify baseline distribution shapes, detect outlier clusters, and assess bivariate associations.

2. Data Provenance & Structure

Data were compiled and curated from official, publicly accessible multilateral research repositories:

  • World Bank Open Data (data.worldbank.org): GDP Per Capita (current USD), Total Population (millions), and Adult Literacy Rate (% of population aged 15 and above).
  • World Health Organization (WHO) Global Health Observatory (who.int/data/gho): Life Expectancy at birth (years) and Healthcare Expenditure (% of national GDP).

The structured dataset contains 50 complete observations across 7 standardized columns, containing zero missing values (NA count = 0), verified for immediate execution in RStudio via the companion analysis.R script and the delivered Excel workbook life_expectancy_data.xlsx.

4. Distributional Characteristics & Regional Disparities

An examination of numeric distribution properties reveals substantial skewness in global health outcomes. Figures 1 through 3 illustrate the global frequency distribution, dispersion patterns across geographic blocs, and rank comparisons of continental averages.

Figure 1: Histogram of life expectancy (binwidth = 3 years). The dashed vertical line denotes the grand mean of 74.9 years. Notice the left skew with a prolonged tail.
Figure 2: Boxplot displaying median, interquartile range (IQR), and spread across regions. Jittered points display raw observations within each continental grouping.

Distributional Properties & Outlier Diagnostics

As visualized in Figure 1, global life expectancy exhibits pronounced left-skewness. A high concentration of countries clusters in the 75–84 year range (mode at 81–84 years). The lower tail extends down to 54.3 years (Nigeria) and 59.3 years (Mozambique), creating a 30.5-year gap between the highest and lowest countries in the sample. The sample median (76.6 years) sits notably above the sample mean (74.88 years), a classic property of negatively skewed public health data where a minority of distressed nations pull down the arithmetic average.

The regional boxplot in Figure 2 reveals sharp geographical stratifications:

  • Europe & Oceania: Demonstrate high medians (>82 years) and remarkably tight interquartile spreads, indicative of universally mature healthcare systems and consistent socioeconomic safety nets.
  • Americas: Median life expectancy reaches 77.0 years. The United States exhibits high life expectancy (79.1 years) but is paired with unprecedented healthcare spending (16.6% of GDP), behaving as an expenditure outlier.
  • Asia: Exhibits the broadest intra-regional variance, ranging from highly developed societies (Japan at 84.8 years, South Korea at 83.7 years) down to emerging economies (Pakistan at 66.1 years).
  • Africa: Records both the lowest regional median (63.4 years) and the highest internal dispersion (standard deviation = 5.56 years), with northern states (Morocco at 74.0 yrs) performing substantially above sub-Saharan nations.
Figure 3: Horizontal bar chart of regional mean life expectancies. A distinct 18.3-year gap separates Oceania from Africa.

Regional Gap Summary

Figure 3 summarizes the stark continental divide: European (80.6 yrs) and Oceanian (82.7 yrs) citizens live on average 16 to 18 years longer than citizens in African nations (64.4 yrs).

Key Observation: The regional difference is strongly paired with economic resources: the average European GDP/capita ($47,381) in the sample is more than 20 times greater than that of the sampled African nations ($2,266).

5. Socioeconomic Associations & Correlation Analysis

Bivariate relationships were evaluated between life expectancy and economic prosperity, healthcare expenditure, and adult literacy. All three factors show positive, statistically robust associations.

Figure 4: Scatter plot of Life Expectancy vs. GDP Per Capita with logarithmic trend curve (r = 0.694). Diminishing returns appear above $30,000.
Figure 5: Life Expectancy vs. Healthcare Expenditure as % of GDP with linear fit (r = 0.661). Higher spending aligns with longer life.
Figure 6: Life Expectancy vs. Adult Literacy Rate with linear fit (r = 0.785). Strongest single linear bivariate predictor in the dataset.

The Preston Curve Effect: In Figure 4, the relationship with GDP per capita is distinctly concave. At early development stages (GDP < $10,000), marginal economic increases correspond to steep gains in lifespan. Beyond $35,000, longevity plateaus between 81 and 85 years.

6. Discussion & Methodological Scope

The findings corroborate established developmental economics: basic income, sanitary infrastructure, and general literacy constitute essential foundations for maternal health and infectious disease containment. Literacy (r = 0.785) shows the highest direct linear correlation with longevity, reflecting how maternal education and health literacy directly influence community survival.

Methodological Scope & Causal Disclaimer: This study is strictly descriptive and cross-sectional. The observed positive correlations (r = 0.66 to 0.79) do not establish causal relationships. Confounding factors—such as institutional quality, environmental conditions, and peacetime stability—simultaneously influence both economic metrics and health outcomes.

7. Conclusions

  • Socioeconomic Underpinnings: Life expectancy is tightly bound to national economic capacity and human development, with literacy rate being the strongest linear covariate.
  • Non-Linear Economic Returns: Wealth yields massive life gains in developing nations, but exhibits diminishing marginal returns in high-income states.
  • Closing the Global Gap: Addressing the 18.3-year lifespan disparity between Africa and advanced economies requires integrated investments in both public healthcare capacity and foundational education.
